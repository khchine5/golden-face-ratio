The method

Ten proportions, measured the old way — but with the same eyes on every face

A ruler on a printed photo gives you different numbers twice. We detect 468 landmarks on your face, correct for the tilt of your head using the eye line, and measure each proportion in source-image pixels. Same photo in, same numbers out.

Where the number comes from

Why anyone thought a face should follow a rule

Euclid described the proportion around 300 BC as a curiosity in geometry. Everything after that is a story about what that number was supposed to mean for people.

c. 300 BC

Euclid

Defines the “extreme and mean ratio” in the Elements. Pure geometry — no talk of beauty at all.

1202

Fibonacci

Publishes the rabbit sequence. The ratio of consecutive terms converges on 1.618, giving the ratio a name and a foothold in nature.

1509

Pacioli & da Vinci

De Divina Proportione ties the ratio to the human body and to composition, illustrated by Leonardo.

1528

Dürer

Four Books on Human Proportion measures real people rather than idealised ones — and finds the ratio showing up often.

1865–76

Fechner

Asks whether the golden section has any claim on taste, then runs the first experiments on aesthetic preference: people choose between rectangles of different shapes. The golden rectangle wins, often.

Today

Landmark models

Google’s MediaPipe finds 468 points on a face in milliseconds, which makes the whole argument measurable — and testable.

The evidence

What the research actually says about golden ratios and faces

Here is the short version, with the sources at the bottom of the section. We would rather you know where the evidence is thin than get a beautiful number and a false story.

The story usually starts with Fechner. He raised the question in 1865, and by 1876 he was showing people rectangles and asking which they preferred; the golden rectangle came out on top often enough to look like a law of taste. A century and a half later, in 1995, Christopher Green went back through the entire literature and reached a more careful conclusion: there probably is a real psychological effect attached to the golden section, but it is sensitive to sloppy methods. It survives careful work and evaporates under careless work. That single sentence is the fairest summary of the field.

When the question moves from rectangles to faces, the evidence gets messier, and the popular story gets thinner.

Mixed evidence

The golden ratio is not the number that flatters a face

Pallett, Link & Lee (2010)

These researchers asked a sharper question than “does phi work”: for a given face, is there one spacing of the features that flatters it? Across four experiments, yes — but the winning numbers were not the classical ones. Attractiveness peaked when the distance from the eyes to the mouth was about 36% of face length, and when the distance between the eyes was about 46% of face width. Both differ significantly from the 0.38 the golden section predicts.

Their new ratios match an average face, which points at a result that has been robust for decades: composite faces, built by averaging many real ones, tend to be rated more attractive than the individuals they came from. “Attractive faces are only average” was the title of the 1990 paper that established it.

Mixed evidence

Proportion explains surprisingly little

Kiekens et al. (2008) · Rossetti et al. (2013) · Zwahlen et al. (2022)

In adolescents, Kiekens and colleagues tested nineteen candidate golden proportions against beauty ratings. Only four of the nineteen correlated with the ratings, and together they accounted for about 16% of the variance — which the authors themselves called too small to matter clinically.

A 2013 study went further and used 3D stereophotogrammetry, the gold standard for facial anthropometry, on 400 people, then compared ten ratios with 1.618. Seven of the ten came out statistically different from the golden value, and none of the ratios tracked how attractive a panel of dental professionals judged the face to be. A 2022 study in Hong Kong Chinese faces reached a similar conclusion: most “ideal” measurements differed significantly from the recommended targets, and only a handful of parameters actually tracked attractiveness ratings.

The popular version fails

The “phi mask” does not describe an ideal face

Holland (2008)

The most widely reproduced version of the golden-ratio face is Stephen Marquardt’s phi mask, a face outline drawn from the ratio in the early 2000s. It is handsome, it is everywhere, and it does not hold up. Holland checked the fit properly, using Procrustes analysis rather than the eyeballing the mask was usually judged by, and found the mask describes the proportions of masculinised white female fashion models far better than any other group. It fits sub-Saharan African and East Asian faces poorly, and its proportions run against what most people actually prefer.

No clear link

Systematic reviews keep landing on the same uncomfortable answer

Londono et al. (2024)

A 2024 systematic review in the Journal of Prosthetic Dentistry searched PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus and the Cochrane database for work on the golden proportion in natural facial esthetics. Its conclusion: no significant association was found between the golden ratio and facial evaluation scores across ethnicities. Other cross-population work — Malaysian and multiracial samples — finds the same thing: the proportions that count as “golden” shift with the population you measure.

Proportion is one input among several. Symmetry, averageness, youthfulness cues and sex-specific shape all contribute — and none of them is a formula.

The more useful literature is not about the golden ratio at all. It is about what people actually respond to: faces close to the population average, faces that are bilaterally symmetric, faces with the shape and colouring associated with health and youth. Even that literature is careful with its claims. Perrett and colleagues showed that the “average face” is attractive but not optimally attractive, and that preferences shift between cultures and between sexes. Work on how attractiveness is computed from photographs has found that low-level image properties — the kind of thing you get from lighting and sharpening — move ratings on their own, with no change to the geometry whatsoever.

So what is this tool for? It measures proportions consistently and shows you the numbers, which is a genuinely useful thing to do with a face: it turns a vague sense of “balanced?” into specific distances you can reason about. It is not a verdict, and it was never validated as a beauty scale. The score here is our own arithmetic, tuned so that a face with every ratio exactly on target scores 100 and a face that is 50% off on every ratio scores 0. Real faces cluster in the middle, and that is what you should expect.

Questions

Things people ask

Does a high score mean I am attractive?

No, and we would rather say so than dress it up. The score summarises how closely ten measurements sit on their targets. Attractiveness research points somewhere else entirely: averageness, symmetry, and cues about health and youth. A face can score well here and still not be the face you are used to seeing in the mirror, and the reverse happens too.

Is my photo uploaded anywhere?

No. The detection model is fetched from a public CDN and then runs inside this page. Your image is decoded in the browser, measured, drawn on a canvas, and discarded. There is no server to send it to, no account, and no analytics on your file.

Which photo gives the best result?

A clear, front-facing shot with even light and a neutral expression. Hair out of the way, no heavy filters, and nothing covering the chin or the eyebrows. If the head is tilted, the measurements are corrected for the tilt of the eye line, but a strongly angled face is still harder to measure than a straight one.

How accurate are the numbers?

Every landmark is an estimate from a 2D photograph, so treat the third decimal place as noise. The measurements are internally consistent and repeatable on the same photo, which is what makes them useful, but they are not measurements to three significant figures. They also cannot see depth: a face photographed from slightly off-axis will have its apparent proportions distorted, and no amount of landmark detection fixes that.

Why do most faces land in the middle of the scale?

Because real faces do. The scoring is deliberately unforgiving at the edges: a ratio 50% away from its target contributes nothing, and a face that misses on every ratio cannot score high. A score in the seventies means “proportionally ordinary, nothing extreme”, which is a good description of most faces.

Does the golden ratio work for every ethnicity?

The measurements do. The interpretation does not. Studies in Malaysian, multiracial, Indian and Chinese populations find that the proportions treated as “golden” differ between populations, and a 2024 systematic review found no significant association between the golden ratio and facial evaluation scores across ethnicities. A number is not a standard of beauty.

Where do the twenty sample portraits come from?

CelebA-HQ, the high-quality version of the CelebA face dataset, served through a public Hugging Face mirror. They are used here so you can see the tool work before you upload anything of your own.

Measure the geometry, keep the judgement. The proportions are a way of looking at a face closely, and a good deal of fun to compare with other people. They are not a score card, and the research is honest about that.
